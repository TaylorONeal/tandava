A yoga studio financial model is a place to try a good idea before you move the furniture. **Studio Sprout** turns that idea into a small, sunny game: choose a room, plan six weeks of classes, and discover what gives your studio room to grow.

It’s a playful companion to [What a Yoga Studio Actually Makes](/blog/yoga-studio-profit-and-break-even) and our [studio profitability calculator](/tools/studio-calculator). Same core questions. A smaller sandbox. A few more flowers.

About five minutes. Phone or desktop. No signup, no ticking clock. Just your own little studio and a neighborhood full of possibility.

## Start with a room you can grow into

You have $3,200 in your studio jar and three spaces to choose from: a cozy nook, a sunny loft, or a garden room. Each has a different capacity and a different weekly bill.

The biggest room isn’t automatically the best choice. Neither is the smallest. More space can welcome more visits, but you pay for that space even in a quiet week. A smaller room can make the numbers easier while giving you a different puzzle: when is it worth adding another class?

That’s the central idea in our break-even post. Room size and rent shape the choices that come after them. Here, you can try one lease, play a season, then try another. No moving boxes required.

## Give every dollar a job

A full class is a lovely sight. To understand what it contributes, we also need the average income per visit and the cost of running it.

In our fictional studio, ten visits at $18 bring in $180. After $55 of teacher pay, $125 remains to help cover rent, owner pay, and the rest. That $125 is a **contribution**, not the final profit.

Teacher pay follows your schedule. If you plan a class, the teacher gets paid whether four people come or fourteen. That makes one more visit in a class you’re already running very different from adding a whole new class.

The game lets you see that distinction before you commit. Change the schedule and watch the forecast. Try a different ticket mix. Every choice has a visible consequence, with the arithmetic waiting in the weekly recap.

> Paying yourself belongs in the plan. You’re part of what makes the studio possible.

Studio Sprout includes $300 of weekly owner pay as a cash expense. Its studio-jar result is therefore **not the same measure as the calculator’s EBITDA**. This game follows a short, simplified cash story; the calculator models a more detailed stabilized business.

## Find the rhythm, then add something lovely

Each week, choose one small initiative.

A **bring-a-friend week** gives visits an immediate lift. A **welcoming ritual** adds fewer visits today but more returning visits in later weeks. A **Sunday workshop** brings separate income and separate costs.

These do different jobs. An invitation can help you fill a room now. A ritual has time to compound if you start early. A workshop can contribute toward overhead, but its ticket income isn’t all profit: the workshop costs still need a place in the ledger.

This also connects to [our retention ideas](/blog/studio-retention-levers): helping someone feel welcome and making it easy to return can support the business as well as the community.

Try one season with the same idea every week. In another, keep the room and ticket mix unchanged but switch the initiative. Comparing two small experiments makes the tradeoff easier to see.

## Leave a little breathing room

Your neighborhood has a rhythm too. There’s a quieter holiday week, a mention from a local café, and a festival that brings more visits.

You can see each event before making that week’s plan. There are no surprise penalties. The point is to notice how cash moves when attendance changes and most costs stay put—the seasonality lesson from our financial-model post, compressed into six turns.

A week that uses some of the studio jar isn’t the end of the game. It’s useful information. You still paid the teachers and yourself. Now you can compare a different schedule or ticket mix. If the jar falls below zero, the game shows the extra funding needed and lets you continue experimenting.

Along the way, collect three garden badges: cover a week’s costs, fill at least 80% of available class spaces, and grow a base of returning visits. They’re little milestones, not a verdict on your business.

## What’s inside this yoga studio financial model?

The game’s numbers are invented teaching assumptions, **not industry benchmarks or the calculator’s default scenarios**. Rent and overhead run from $900 to $1,450 per week. Teacher pay is $55 per class. Average realized class income is $14, $18, or $22 per visit, across passes and drop-ins.

Changing ticket mix changes expected visits through a deliberately simple rule. Attendance stops at capacity. Returning visits from an initiative start the following week. Workshop income is separate from regular class visits. Every forecast is exact in this game; a real studio has more uncertainty.

Taxes, payment processing, financing, build-out costs, and membership billing timing sit outside this small model. It also combines peak and off-peak attendance. The full calculator explores more of those details, and [our discovery-platform post](/blog/discovery-platforms-vs-your-own-funnel) explains why the time at which a visit happens matters.
